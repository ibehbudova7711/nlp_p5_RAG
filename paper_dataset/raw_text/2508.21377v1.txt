Abstract—Large Language Models (LLMs) are transforming AI across industries, but their development and deployment remain complex. This survey reviews 16 key challenges
in building and using LLMs and examines how these challenges are addressed by two state-of-the-art models with unique
approaches: OpenAI’s closed-source GPT-4o (May 2024 update) and DeepSeek-V3-0324 (March 2025), a large opensource Mixture-of-Experts model. Through this comparison, we
showcase the trade-offs between closed-source models (robust
safety, fine-tuned reliability) and open-source models (efficiency,
adaptability). We also explore LLM applications across different
domains (from chatbots and coding tools to healthcare and
education),highlighting which model attributes are best suited
for each use case. This article aims to guide AI researchers,
developers, and decision-makers in understanding current LLM
capabilities, limitations, and best practices.
I. I NTRODUCTION
Large Language Models (LLMs) have achieved remarkable
capabilities in natural language processing, enabling applications from chatbots and virtual assistants to code generation
and scientific discovery. With models like OpenAI’s GPT
powering widely used products (e.g. ChatGPT) and open
source alternatives (e.g. DeepSeek, Meta’s LLaMA), there
is growing interest in understanding what challenges these
models face and how different design choices affect their
performance and safety. Recent work by Kaddour et al. [5]
identified a broad landscape of LLM challenges and applications, highlighting issues in data quality, model alignment and
evaluation methodology.
• GPT-4o: a close source model accessible via API from
OpenAI. GPT-4o is an improved version of GPT-4 released in 2024 [12], featuring enhancements like a larger
context window and fine-tuning refinements. It benefits
from OpenAI’s extensive resources for training and alignment (using techniques such as reinforcement learning
from human feedback) and operates within a controlled
environment (the model’s parameters and training data
are not publicly released).
• DeepSeek: an open source LLM released in March 2025
by a research consortium (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025).
It uses a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture with
671 billion parameters (of which ∼37B are active per
token) and was trained on 14.8 trillion tokens. DeepSeek
showcases efficiency and transparency: the model weights
are openly available and it achieved high performance
at a fraction of the training cost of GPT-4 (however its
training data is not fully disclosed).
This survey reviews 16 key challenges in LLM development and deployment, based on Kaddour et al. (2023),
grouped into three categories: (a) Design challenges: issues in
model building and pre-deployment (e.g. data curation, model
size, compute costs), (b) Behavioral challenges: issues during
model interaction (e.g. prompt brittleness, hallucinations, value
alignment), and (c) Scientific/Evaluation challenges: barriers
to research or benchmarking (e.g. reproducibility, evaluation
bias). For each, we explain the issue, compare how GPT4o and DeepSeek address it, and highlight their respective
strengths and limitations.
We then examine major LLM applications, such as conversational AI, coding, healthcare, law, creative writing and
education to show how these challenges arise in practice. By
linking model traits (e.g. GPT-4o’s alignment vs. DeepSeek’s
openness and efficiency) to use cases, we clarify tradeoffs
between closed source polish and open source transparency.
GPT-4o often leads in safety and reliability, while DeepSeek
offers more flexibility and cost benefits.
Contributions: (1) A unified explanation of 16 core LLM
challenges updated with the latest model insights, (2) a headto-head comparison of GPT-4o and DeepSeek, and (3) practical guidance connecting technical limitations to real-world
model selection and risk mitigation.
II. B ACKGROUND AND PRELIMINARIES
A. LLMs: Transformer and MoE Architecture
Large Language Models (LLMs) leverage dense Transformer or sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures [1],
[2]. Dense Transformers activate all parameters per task, while
sparse MoE models activate only a subset of specialized

experts, thereby reducing compute costs. LLMs are pre-trained
on vast corpora to predict the next token, and later fine-tuned
for specific tasks. Scaling model size and data drove progress:
GPT-3’s 175B parameters enabled few-shot learning [3], but
high costs prompted efforts toward computing-optimal training
to balance size and data [4].
B. GPT-4o
GPT-4o, released by OpenAI in May 2024, is a dense
Transformer-based LLM with an estimated several hundred
billion parameters. Figure 1 presents an illustrative architectural schematic of GPT-4o. Key features include:
• Multimodality: Processes text and images with potential
audio support, surpassing GPT-4.
• Context Window: Handles 128,000 tokens (4× GPT-4’s
32,000) for long-context tasks.
• Alignment: Uses reinforcement learning from human
feedback (RLHF) [7] and adversarial training to improve
instruction adherence and safety, reducing disallowed
content by 82%.
• Performance: Achieves 89% accuracy (vs. GPT-4’s
84%), 87% precision (vs. 82%), with 2× faster inference
and 50% lower cost.
Accessible via OpenAI’s API, GPT-4o’s closed model limits
insight into its weights but ensures continuous updates.

C. DeepSeek-V3-0324
DeepSeek-V3-0324, released in March, 2025 by DeepSeekAI, is an open-source, sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) LLM
with 671B total parameters, activating approximately 37B per
token [11]. This gives it efficiency similar to a 30–40B dense
model. Key innovations include:
• Multi-Head Latent Attention (MLA): Compresses keyvalue states for a 128,000-token context window with low
memory usage.
• 8-bit Precision: Uses FP8 training for significant compute savings.
• Multi-Token Prediction (MTP): Predicts multiple tokens in parallel to accelerate training.
Figure 2 illustrates the detailed architecture of
DeepSeek-V3-0324. Trained on 14.8T tokens using 2.788
million H800 GPU hours (approximately $5–6M), DeepSeek
is cost-efficient compared to GPT-4o (approximately $100M).
Fine-tuning was layered on top of earlier models: DeepSeekR1 for reasoning, DeepSeek-V2.5 for creative writing and
used Grouped Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) on 1.5M
human rated prompts for alignment. Open weights enable
community adaptation and experimentation. DeepSeek shows
strong performance in tasks involving coding and math,
though safety enforcement is left to downstream users.

By GPT-4o, we mean the enhanced GPT-4 model released by
OpenAI in May 2024. By DeepSeek, we refer specifically to
DeepSeek-V3-0324, released in March 2025.

A. Unfathomable Datasets
LLMs are trained on vast, often unexamined datasets
(trillions of tokens), which risks the absorption of biases,
copyrighted content, or test answers. The term unfathomable
reflects the inability to manually audit such data.
GPT-4o (Closed): GPT-4o mitigates risks from opaque
datasets through a multi-layered safety framework. Posttraining alignment via Reinforcement Learning from Human
Feedback (RLHF) guides the model away from harmful or
biased outputs. Content filtering APIs block unsafe responses,

and the model refuses sensitive queries (e.g., medical advice).
Red-teaming and safety audits identify vulnerabilities, while
instruction fine-tuning on curated datasets enhances reasoning
and coherence. Ongoing data provenance research aims to
improve transparency. These measures reduce downstream
risks, though dataset opacity persists.
DeepSeek (Open): DeepSeek, pre-trained on 14.8T tokens,
uses a curated data mix emphasizing math, programming, and
multilingual content to minimize redundancy. Post-training
employs a multi-stage curation process: Supervised FineTuning (SFT) data for reasoning is generated by DeepSeekR1 and filtered via rejection sampling, while creative writing
data from DeepSeek-V2.5 is human-verified. However, the
pretraining dataset remains a ‘black box’, and hence the
model’s internal biases and potential for memorization are
difficult to audit. Documented censorship reveals inherent
biases, challenging transparency claims.
Comparison: GPT-4o adopts a reactive approach, using
extensive safety mechanisms (RLHF, content filters, redteaming) to manage risks from opaque data. DeepSeek focuses on proactive post-training curation with specialized
models but lacks GPT-4o’s robust safety infrastructure. This
highlights the open-weight, closed-data dilemma: DeepSeek’s
open weights (MIT license) allow modification, but its 14.8Ttoken dataset is closed, limiting auditability. In LLMs, training
data acts as source code embedding both biases and knowledge. DeepSeek’s open weights offer deployment freedom but
not full transparency, unlike traditional open-source software,
while GPT-4o’s closed model relies on centralized safety
guardrails.
Verdict: GPT-4o has a significant advantage in mitigating
the downstream risks from unfathomable datasets due to its
mature, extensive, and well-documented safety ecosystem.
DeepSeek’s approach, while innovative in its post-training
curation, cannot compensate for the lack of a comparable
safety infrastructure. This is illustrated in the example prompt
presented in Figure 3.
B. Tokenizer Reliance
LLMs rely on tokenizers to break text into tokens. Poor
tokenization leads to splitting common words inefficiently or
mishandling rare strings which can hurt performance, especially across languages. Any change to the tokenizer often
requires retraining or fine-tuning the entire model.
GPT-4o: GPT addresses these issues through architectural
refinements and a highly efficient tokenizer that reduces token
count. This enhances multilingual support, cuts processing
costs and improves fairness in token-based pricing. GPT4o’s multimodal architecture further reduces reliance on text
tokenization by integrating vision and audio. For images,
hierarchical tokenization captures information at multiple abstraction levels, improving efficiency and representational richness. Together these innovations reduce glitch tokens, decouple
tokenization from embedding constraints and enable scalable,
inclusive model use.
DeepSeek: Uses a Byte-level BPE tokenizer with a
128,000-token vocabulary and multilingual optimizations like
combined punctuation tokens. While compression is efficient,
design flaws remain, such as the lack of a system role token,
which forces all system messages to the beginning of the
prompt. This disrupts instruction following in multiturn chats,
degrading reliability.
Comparison: GPT-4o prioritizes robust, multilingual and
multimodal support, while DeepSeek’s tokenizer favors benchmark efficiency at the cost of conversational reliability. Realworld issues like prompt handling and glitch tokens highlight
the importance of tokenizer quality.
Verdict: GPT-4o can be considered better with a design that
reflects focus on efficiency, equity and robustness and hence
better suited for diverse and practical use cases. Figures 4 and
5 present a comparison of tokenization across the two models, highlighting GPT’s direct responses versus DeepSeek’s
nuanced treatment of ambiguity.
C. High Pretraining Costs
LLMs are computationally expensive to train, costing millions of dollars and emitting large carbon footprints. Scaling
laws indicate diminishing returns beyond certain model sizes.
GPT-4o: Reduces training costs through optimized scaling laws, efficient pretraining objectives like span corruption
and advanced parallelism (data, model and pipeline). These
strategies help make pretraining more scalable and sustainable
without compromising model performance.
DeepSeek: Takes an efficient path using a Mixture-ofExperts (MoE) architecture, activating only ∼37B out of
685B parameters per token. Innovations like Multi-head Latent
Attention (MLA), Multi-Token Prediction (MTP) and FP8
precision further cut computation and memory demands. The
full training of DeepSeek, including pretraining and finetuning, required only 2.788 million H800 GPU hours. This
translates to an estimated training cost of just $5.6 to $6
million. This figure stands in stark contrast to the estimated
costs for models like GPT-4, which are reported to be over
$100 million.
Comparison: While GPT-4o improves efficiency within
traditional paradigms, DeepSeek is a fundamental architectural
and philosophical shift that gives comparable results at a fraction of the cost, offering a compelling model for sustainable
and accessible AI.
Verdict: DeepSeek sets a new standard for performance-tocost ratio, addressing high pretraining costs.

D. Fine-Tuning Overhead
Adapting pre-trained LLMs to specific tasks often requires
updating billions of parameters and substantial GPU memory.
While newer methods like LoRA and prefix tuning reduce this
overhead, not all models support them efficiently. The goal is
to allow customization without retraining the entire model.
GPT-4o: Uses parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods like
Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), adapters and prompt tuning
to update only small subsets of parameters, lowering memory
and compute costs. Techniques like MeZO further reduce GPU
usage by avoiding full gradient storage, lowering hardware
requirements.
DeepSeek: Offers full fine-tuning flexibility thanks to its
open weights and MIT license. It supports LoRA, QLoRA and
prefix tuning even on consumer hardware backed by a growing
ecosystem of community tools and quantized versions. This
openness allows deep customization, academic research and
lightweight deployment, minimizing platform lock-in.
Comparison: GPT-4o favors convenience, with streamlined
fine-tuning for enterprise users. DeepSeek favors control,
offering full customization for researchers and developers. The
trade-off is between simplicity and flexibility.
Verdict: DeepSeek leads in fine-tuning versatility. It empowers users to deeply customize and experiment with the
model, while GPT-4o is ideal for those prioritizing ease of
use and operational simplicity.
E. High Inference Latency
Large models can be slow, especially for interactive use.
Latency is affected by model size, context length and hardware. Reducing it requires both architectural and engineering
optimizations.
GPT-4o: Delivers major latency gains through architectural
streamlining and system level improvements. Its unified multimodal design eliminates overhead from separate subsystems,
while memory and tokenizer optimizations accelerate token
processing. GPT-4o is reportedly twice as fast and 50%
cheaper than GPT-4 Turbo, with sub second response times
enabled by backend enhancements like batching, caching and
parallelism. These gains are proven at scale across millions of
real-world interactions.
DeepSeek: Built for speed using innovations like Multihead Latent Attention (which reduces KV cache size) and
Mixture-of-Experts (activating fewer parameters per token).
Multi-Token Prediction allows speculative decoding, accelerating generation under favorable conditions. On high end
hardware, it can reach up to 250 tokens per sec. However,
third party tests report a more moderate average 27.6 tokens
per sec with 2.43s TTFT highlighting performance variance
based on environment and tuning.
Comparison: GPT-4o offers consistent low latency performance via end to end system optimization in a tightly
integrated ecosystem. DeepSeek has higher theoretical speed
on optimized hardware but more variability in real world
settings.
Verdict: DeepSeek excels in speed potential for users with
custom deployments and hardware control. GPT-4o delivers
more predictable and fast performance out of the box for most
users.
F . Limited Context Length
LLMs can only attend to a fixed length context window.
Even with large limits (e.g. 100K+ tokens), models often struggle to effectively utilize the full range, particularly information
located in the middle of long input contexts [14] .
GPT-4o: Expands its context window to 128K tokens
and demonstrates consistent performance across that range.
It maintains coherence in long, multi-part conversations and
dense documents without truncation or chunking. GPT-4o
mitigates the “lost in the middle” problem through undisclosed
but effective architectural tuning, enabling accurate reasoning
over full-length inputs. This supports complex use cases like
legal analysis, academic summarization and long code reviews
by preserving continuity and context awareness.
DeepSeek: Advertises 128K–164K token support but shows
steep performance drops after ∼20K tokens. Users report
vague or erroneous outputs beyond mid-range contexts and
outright failures at higher lengths ( ∼56K tokens). The model
frequently forgets or misinterprets mid-context content, limiting its utility in long-context reasoning. Despite its theoretical
capacity, DeepSeek’s practical context handling falls short,
with limited architectural mitigation of the “lost in the middle”
issue.
Comparison: Both claim 128K token capacity but only
GPT-4o reliably delivers strong performance across the full
window. DeepSeek’s sharp performance drop restricts its effective usage to shorter contexts, especially in high stakes
applications.
Verdict: GPT-4o is the clear winner for long-context tasks.
It handles extensive inputs with high accuracy and minimal
degradation, unlike DeepSeek, which struggles well below its
stated limits.

GPT-4o: Addresses prompt brittleness through fine-tuning

under optimal conditions, is more brittle and sensitive to
prompt formatting and temperature settings.
Verdict: GPT-4o is more robust to prompt variation. It’s
optimized for general-purpose reliability, while DeepSeek requires precise prompting and tuning, making it less adaptable
for everyday use.
H. Hallucinations
LLMs often generate plausible but false information. Reducing these is essential for tasks requiring factual correctness,
such as medical advice, legal analysis or academic Q&A.
GPT-4o: Employs a multi layered approach to reducing
hallucinations. At its core is reinforcement learning from
human feedback (RLHF), which optimizes the model using
reward signals derived from human preferences. This is paired
with supervised fine-tuning on datasets emphasizing factual
correctness and task adherence (e.g. summarization). Additionally, GPT-4o integrates retrieval augmented generation (RAG)
[10], enabling it to draw from external sources like web
results or databases. Architectural upgrades such as enhanced
attention and decoding strategies (e.g. beam search, Bayes
risk decoding) further support accurate outputs. GPT-4o is
trained to express uncertainty, acknowledge knowledge cutoffs
and decline to answer when unsure reducing the chance of
confident but incorrect completions. Together, these features
make it a reliable LLM in terms of factual grounding.
DeepSeek: Uses a lighter alignment pipeline focused on
supervised fine-tuning and rule based RL. While it performs
well in domains with objective ground truth (e.g. mathematical
calculations, coding), its open-domain factual accuracy is
more limited. According to Vectara’s HHEM 2.1 benchmark,
DeepSeek has a hallucination rate of 3.9%, compared to GPT4o’s 1.5%. DeepSeek-R1, which is reasoning optimized, fared
worse at 14.3%. DeepSeek is designed with retrieval integration in mind and performs best when paired with external
context. However, in standalone scenarios without retrieval, it
is more prone to hallucinations, especially when attempting
to reason around knowledge gaps. Its alignment lacks the full
RLHF depth of GPT-4o and is more brittle when handling
uncertainty or open-ended prompts.
Comparison: GPT-4o achieves lower hallucination due to
deeper alignment, tool integration and self regulation behaviors. It is safer and more trustworthy for standalone factual
tasks. DeepSeek, while competitive in retrieval-augmented
environments, is riskier in use without external grounding.
Verdict: GPT-4o is superior at mitigating hallucinations
out-of-the-box. Its lower intrinsic hallucination rate makes it
the safer choice for applications that demand high factual
accuracy.
I. Misaligned Behavior
Poorly aligned LLMs can produce unsafe or unethical
outputs, including misinformation, hate speech and responses
to dangerous prompts. Ensuring alignment with human values
is critical, especially in public or regulated applications.
GPT-4o: Uses a comprehensive alignment pipeline rooted
in RLHF, instruction fine-tuning and safety first training
practices. Human annotators provide feedback across safety
relevant scenarios, which is then used to build reward models
and fine-tune responses through PPO. GPT-4o also undergoes
intensive adversarial testing via red teaming, allowing it to
withstand edge case attacks and trick prompts. Chain-ofthought and self-consistency prompting strategies minimize
risky outputs. Additionally, GPT-4o employs self-refinement
loops [13] , multi-pass generation and internal critique mechanisms to improve safety and correctness in complex responses.
These measures, combined with a closed deployment model,
enable OpenAI to monitor and update the model continually,
ensuring it meets high ethical and compliance standards.
DeepSeek: As an open-weight model, DeepSeek offers
minimal built-in safety guarantees. Independent audits have
exposed numerous vulnerabilities. It failed all tested Pliny
prompt injections and exhibited a 77% success rate for
prompt attacks and 69.2% for evasion techniques. It also
underperformed on harmful content benchmarks: only 35.6%
pass on WMD-related prompts, 53.3% on hate speech and
48.9% on self-harm. In one notable failure, it provided detailed instructions for processing cocaine, an outcome GPT-4o
would strongly reject. DeepSeek relies primarily on GRPO
for alignment and human verified fine-tuning but lacks the

[PAGE 10]
broader RLHF pipeline and safety investment seen in GPT4o. Moreover, its open architecture makes it easy to fine-tune
or jailbreak, meaning alignment is effectively at the discretion
of the end user.
Comparison: GPT-4o’s alignment is robust, deeply integrated into its architecture and deployment. Its behavior
remains stable across use cases, thanks to red teaming, tool
use and human in the loop feedback. DeepSeek lacks comparable safeguards and is vulnerable to exploitation. While its
openness allows flexibility, it also exposes significant safety
risks.
Verdict: GPT-4o is clearly superior in safety and alignment.
It offers strong resistance to prompt injection, ethical safeguards and better refusal behavior, making it a responsible
choice for any application with the potential for public interaction or misuse. Figures 6 and 7 illustrate the differing safety
responses of the two models, with GPT consistently rejecting
harmful or biased prompts, while DeepSeek occasionally
generated detailed outputs when the bias was less explicit.
J. Outdated Knowledge
LLMs have a knowledge cutoff. They don’t automatically
incorporate facts or events that occur after training. Given how
quickly the world changes, models must have mechanisms to
remain current.
GPT-4o: GPT-4o mitigates outdated knowledge through a
combination of retrieval augmented generation and system
level tools. It can dynamically access external sources, such as
through the browsing feature in ChatGPT to provide accurate,
up to date responses. It also employs optimized prompting and
retrieval chains to surface relevant information and supports
continual learning frameworks for efficient model editing
and incremental updates. While the exact training data is
undisclosed, the model appears to include recent events via
fine-tuning and plugin integrations. When tools are disabled
or information is unavailable, GPT-4o typically acknowledges
its limitations, reducing the risk of hallucinations. Overall, it
offers a seamless, integrated solution for addressing knowledge
gaps.
DeepSeek: DeepSeek is explicitly designed for integration
with external tools and retrieval systems. It has a static
knowledge cutoff, likely March 2024, inferred from its ‘0324’
version name but is intended to function as a reasoning
engine over live data rather than a standalone knowledge
base. The model is optimized for use in RAG pipelines
and official documentation includes prompt templates for
integrating real-time web search. Advanced frameworks like
QuantMCP demonstrate its ability to interpret external API
data effectively. As an open weight model, DeepSeek can also
be fine-tuned or connected to proprietary databases, though
this requires developer effort.
Comparison: Both models address the challenge by augmenting static training with dynamic retrieval. The primary
difference lies in the implementation and user experience.
GPT-4o offers this capability as a polished, seamlessly integrated product. For example, the browsing feature in ChatGPT
allows the model to access the live web with no complex
setup required from the user. This provides a powerful, outof-the-box solution. DeepSeek provides the powerful, openweight reasoning engine and expects the developer to build
the RAG pipeline or tool-use framework around it. This
offers far greater flexibility and customization but requires
more engineering effort. Developers can choose their own
retrieval systems, data sources and integration logic, tailoring
the solution to their specific needs.
Verdict: The models are strategically aligned in their approach to this challenge. GPT-4o excels in ease of use and
user experience with its built-in tools, while DeepSeek offers
a highly adaptable and open solution for developers building
custom retrieval or tool augmented applications.
K. Brittle Evaluations
Standard NLP benchmarks become less reliable as models
approach saturation, sometimes by exploiting dataset data
leakage. Small changes in prompts can shift performance,
making benchmarks brittle indicators of real world capability.
GPT-4o: While GPT-4o performs well across many benchmarks, OpenAI has emphasized avoiding overfitting. The
model was not fine-tuned on public benchmark datasets,
reducing train on test risks. Its evaluation involved more robust strategies, including HELM-style assessments, adversarial
prompts and hidden test sets. GPT-4o’s training likely incorporated diverse prompt styles through instruction tuning, chainof-thought prompting which bring robustness. It typically
responds consistently even with varied phrasings and OpenAI
actively encourages third-party testing to surface brittleness
not caught internally.
DeepSeek: DeepSeek shows strong performance on specific
benchmarks like AIME and LiveCodeBench, reflecting likely
targeted optimization. However, this focus can lead to brittleness in novel formats. For instance, earlier DeepSeek versions
struggled on the Misguided Attention Eval, which requires
filtering out distractions, though version 0324 showed marked
improvement due to adaptation rather than generalization.
Its outputs remain sensitive to system prompt design and
performance can vary accordingly. Still, DeepSeek’s openness
enables researchers to test and improve the model through
adversarial prompting and fine-tuning, even if this risks overfitting to new benchmarks.
Comparison: GPT-4o’s breadth of real world exposure
and diverse prompt training enhances generalization, reducing
benchmark brittleness. DeepSeek’s optimization may lead to

L. Evaluations Based on Static, Human-Written Ground Truth
Many benchmarks use fixed sets of human written answers
or few shots, which can be incomplete or biased. LLMs might
produce correct answers not in the key or might perform better
if allowed to explain or if judged differently.
GPT-4o: GPT employs dynamic evaluation methods that go
beyond rigid, static benchmarks. In complex or open-ended
tasks, OpenAI often uses human evaluations or model-in-theloop methods, for example prompting a model to assess the
correctness and clarity of another model’s answer. This helps
address cases where the model’s output is valid but phrased
differently from a reference answer. GPT-4o is also fine-tuned
on instructional datasets and trained to align with human
preferences through RLHF, which encourages responses that
are high-quality even if they don’t match ground truth exactly.
It likely benefits from exposure to diverse answer formulations
during training, helping it produce robust and well-reasoned
outputs. This dynamic alignment helps the model generalize
beyond any single static benchmark key.
DeepSeek: DeepSeek employs a model-centric training
approach that directly addresses the limitations of static ground
truth. Its post training process incorporates data generated
by other models-complex reasoning data from DeepSeek-R1
and general instruction data from DeepSeek-V2.5. This data
is curated using human annotators or rule based systems,
producing a training corpus richer than what could be achieved
through static human-written examples alone. The alignment
process also includes knowledge distillation from the more
capable DeepSeek-R1 model, transferring advanced reasoning
skills to DeepSeek. Additionally, the model uses rule-based
and model-based reward systems to reinforce high quality
outputs, moving further away from fixed answer keys and
toward dynamic self improvement. Because DeepSeek is open,
researchers can adapt evaluation scripts to accommodate alternate phrasings, partial credit or creative reasoning paths. This
flexibility makes the model suitable for domains where strict
matching fails to capture true performance.
Comparison: Both GPT-4o and DeepSeek are pushing
beyond static evaluation limitations. GPT-4o integrates RLHF,
dynamic evaluation and robust training to produce high-quality
outputs even in the absence of perfect benchmark alignment.
DeepSeek explicitly leverages model-generated training data
and transparent reward model pipelines to overcome the same
challenge, with added openness for external evaluation and
adaptation.
Verdict: Both models demonstrate state-of-the-art solutions
to the limitations of static ground truth. GPT-4o benefits
from human preference alignment and dynamic evaluation
methods, while DeepSeek offers a well-documented, model
generated data pipeline and openness that enables deeper
experimentation.
M. Indistinguishability between Generated and HumanWritten Text
LLM generated text can be so fluent that it’s hard to tell
apart from human written text. This raises concerns about
misinformation, academic dishonesty, etc. and there’s interest
in methods to detect AI text or watermark it [8].
GPT-4o: OpenAI has explored watermarking techniques
that subtly embed signals into model outputs to help distinguish AI-generated content. While it’s unclear if these are
applied by default, the closed nature of GPT-4o’s deployment
allows OpenAI to enable such features in specific contexts,
such as educational or regulatory use cases. Additionally, there
are detection tools [9] that estimate whether a model like
GPT-4o produced a given passage, though these tools are not
foolproof, especially when outputs are paraphrased. OpenAI
has emphasized proactive safety and misuse prevention over
watermarking. However, it has a centralized infrastructure
that allows for tracing and detection features to be integrated
behind the scenes. If needed, these could be applied broadly
without exposing the underlying mechanism.
DeepSeek: The open-weight nature of DeepSeek makes this
challenge more difficult to address. The model’s documentation does not mention watermarking or built in detection
features. Since the weights are fully open, any embedded
watermark could be identified, modified or removed. Open
formats like safetensors help with security but offer no mechanism for generation tracing. In practice, DeepSeek’s output
resembles those of other open models and generic detectors
may struggle to reliably flag its content as AI generated,
especially after paraphrasing or post processing. Since users
have full control, they can fine-tune the model or adjust outputs
to further reduce AI-detection cues. The existence of models
like DeepSeek means watermarking alone cannot solve the
detection problem and hence bad actors could always turn to
open alternatives.
Comparison: This is a challenge shaped by deployment
strategy. GPT-4o benefits from a centralized setup that supports detection and watermarking infrastructure. DeepSeek’s
openness maximizes transparency and flexibility but makes
enforceable tracing nearly impossible.
Verdict: GPT-4o has a clear advantage in this area. Its
closed, centralized architecture enables optional watermarking
and more robust detection options. DeepSeek, while open and
powerful, cannot support such mechanisms once its weights
are released.

N. Tasks Not Solvable by Scale Alone
Larger models do not always perform better and they
perform worse on certain tasks involving moral reasoning,
logical nuance or common sense traps. These are examples
where more data and parameters amplify problematic heuristics rather than fix them.
GPT-4o: OpenAI observed inverse scaling effects in earlier
models such as increased toxicity or misplaced confidence as
models grew. GPT-4o counters this by using Reinforcement
Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), counterfactual training data and fine-tuning to shape model behavior, especially
in areas like truthfulness and moral reasoning. For example,
it was trained to say ‘I don’t know‘ rather than fabricate false
answers. OpenAI also applied chain-of-thought prompting,
turning some inverse scaling tasks into learnable patterns by
encouraging step-by-step reasoning. Flawed heuristics were
corrected through curated datasets and alignment strategies,
showing that scale was necessary but not sufficient and targeted interventions were essential.
DeepSeek: DeepSeek’s design explicitly tackles the limits
of scale by augmenting training data with math and code and
applying knowledge distillation from DeepSeek-R1, a reasoning oriented model. Its Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture
allows sub-models to specialize in tasks like logic puzzles or
multi step math. Fine-tuning and architectural tuning led to
strong performance on evaluations like Misguided Attention
Eval, which often confound large language models. For hallucination prone tasks, DeepSeek is also trained to produce
conservative responses.
Comparison: Both models acknowledge the limits of scale.
GPT-4o applies alignment and data-centric fixes for general
purpose reasoning and truthfulness, while DeepSeek emphasizes architecture level specialization and targeted training in
logic heavy domains.
Verdict: DeepSeek shines in this area. Its architecture
and training strategy are purpose-built to handle reasoning
tasks where scale alone fails. GPT-4o also performs well
after alignment but DeepSeek’s targeted innovations make it
particularly strong in domains like logic and math.
O. Lacking Experimental Designs
Many state of the art models lack detailed documentation
on what architectural choices, training strategies or dataset
modifications led to performance gains.
GPT-4o: OpenAI disclosed little about GPT-4o’s internal
design, citing safety and competition concerns. Its technical report lacked details on model size, training procedures, dataset
composition or architectural experiments. Hence researchers
cannot study what contributed most to GPT-4o’s performance.
While OpenAI provides evaluation tools (e.g. OpenAI Evals),
the model itself remains a black box. Even widely speculated
aspects like how multimodal training influenced text output
remain unconfirmed.
DeepSeek: In contrast, DeepSeek released a detailed technical report, including ablation studies for core innovations like
Multi-Token Prediction (MTP) and MoE load balancing. They
discussed not only strengths but also observed limitations.
With open weights and an MIT license, researchers can inspect
components, test new hypotheses or run their own ablations
such as disabling experts or modifying training objectives.
DeepSeek treats its model as a research artifact, not just a
product.
Comparison: This is a clear win for DeepSeek. OpenAI’s
closeness protects product integrity and safety but limits
external validation. DeepSeek embraces scientific openness,
allowing researchers to analyze, test and build upon its work.
Verdict: DeepSeek’s open-weight philosophy is a major
step forward for addressing the lack of experimental designs in
the field. It provides a level of transparency and accessibility
that is absent in the close source ecosystem. For academic
progress and scientific inquiry, DeepSeek is a powerful resource.
P . Lack of Reproducibility
Reproducibility is essential to scientific progress but difficult
to achieve in closed source or frequently updated systems. If
models change silently or cannot be re-run, published results
may not hold overtime.
GPT-4o: GPT-4o is only available via API. OpenAI provides versioned endpoints (e.g. gpt-4-turbo), allowing users to
specify the model used for an experiment. However, older versions will eventually deprecate and underlying model changes
can affect reproducibility over time. Researchers cannot retrain
or inspect the model, nor verify internal claims independently.
This yields practical reproducibility in the short term but
weakens long-term scientific traceability.
DeepSeek: In theory, having access to the exact model
weights allows for perfect reproducibility. Any user can download the 0324 checkpoint and with the correct setup, should
be able to replicate results exactly. However, reproducing
results is technically demanding, requiring massive compute,
consistent software stacks and exact prompt formatting. Even
small variations in tokenization or hardware can cause output
drift.
Comparison: GPT-4o offers consistent results for most
users via versioned APIs but lacks transparency and long term
reproducibility guarantees. DeepSeek demands more setup and
infrastructure but offers true reproducibility for those with the
dedicated infrastructure required to control the entire inference
environment.

[PAGE 15]
Verdict: For academic and experimental reproducibility,
DeepSeek is superior. GPT-4o is better suited for consistent,
production grade use but its closed nature prevents full replication or verification of research findings.
IV. A PPLICATIONS OF LLM S: CHOOSING BETWEEN
GPT-4 O AND DEEP SEEK
After analyzing and comparing GPT-4o and DeepSeek with
respect to technical challenges in the above sections, we
will be talking about how the differences translate in various
applications across multiple domains. We will be comparing
each model’s pros and cons to determine the better fit.
A. Conversational Chatbots and Virtual Assistants
Use case: In this scenario AI agents interact with users in
regular language on open-ended topics viz. customer support
bots, personal assistants, etc.
Recommendation: GPT-4o wins this as it is usually safer
and more reliable choice in such scenarios.
Rationale: Chatbots interact with regular users regarding
their queries and are expected to handle unpredictable inputs
(with ref to Challenge 3.7 prompt brittleness and section
3.9 alignment are the foremost priorities). GPT-4o’s strong
alignment implies it will refuse politely or safely handle toxic
or dangerous prompts as they are crucial to avoid mishaps. It
also maintains consistency in its tone and responses as per its
instruction fine tuning and handles follow-up questions coherently (it stores the extended context and conversation history in
its memory to address Challenge 3.6). While DeepSeek cannot
be sidelined, it can serve as a chatbot and it being an open
source model, it can be customized and fine tuned to a domain
(e.g. IT/healthcare/finance etc.) but there is a relatively higher
risk of producing off the context or unsafe responses unless
strictly monitored or fine-tuned for safety.
B. Content Creation (Writing, Marketing, Creative Work)
Use case: Known for generating articles, creative stories,
tips and suggestions etc.
Recommendation: GPT-4o is usually preferred for creative
writing owing to its refined style and high safety benchmark.
If more freedom is needed on sensitive content DeepSeek can
be employed.
Rationale: Creative writing needs a diverse tone, humor
and depth. In such scenarios GPT-4o is the go to solution as
it has an edge on the RLHF training with respect to emotional
intelligence, making it better for heartfelt or imaginative
writing. DeepSeek does a decent job as well but is slightly
biased with clarity and logic which might come at the expense
of flair and depth needed in creative and imaginative writing.
A hybrid approach seems viable as for generating raw ideas
DeepSeek does a good job with added cost effectiveness and
quick responses and these outputs can be refined with GPT4o. But if we are supposed to use it for polished public
content,GPT-4o is the safer and best option.
C. Data Analysis and Summarization
Use case: Summarizing documents, analyzing trends in text
data and for supporting literature reviews.
Recommendation: GPT-4o is best for most summarization
and analysis tasks owing to its longer context window, consistent and reliable output. DeepSeek is preferred for shorter
inputs or internal use and demands more supervision.
Rationale: The strong factual accuracy (Challenge 3.8) is
well handled by GPT-4o as it can process large inputs (up
to 128k tokens) and produces coherent, precise and concise
summaries. Best suited for summarizing lengthy reports or for
extracting insights from emails. While DeepSeek also supports
longer contexts, quality may be an issue with very large inputs.
For moderate length content it performs decently although
chunking might be required. Generally, summaries from GPT4o are more structured and better user aligned due to its fine
tuning with feedback. For more sensitive analysis something
like legal docs, GPT-4o’s reliability is a plus. DeepSeek, on
the other hand can be deployed on premise making it good
for confidential data but necessary caution must be taken to
review outputs more carefully.
D. Scientific Research and Mathematical Problem-Solving
Use case: For solving math problems, analyzing scientific
papers, suggesting hypotheses.
Recommendation: DeepSeek is preferred for common reasoning and math-heavy tasks. GPT-4o is better for general
scientific comprehension and seamless explanation.
Rationale: DeepSeek’s roots in reasoning models (like R1)
makes it strong for logic and math related scenarios often outperforming on benchmarks like MATH and LiveCodeBench. It
is also open weight, allowing seamless integration into custom
research workflows (e.g. symbolic solvers, proof generation).
GPT-4o on the other hand is relatively less specialized although offers excellent language understanding with low hallucination rates well suited for summarizing complex research
or proposing experiments. DeepSeek can parse dense material
and be fine-tuned for domain specific terminology but caution
needs to be taken as factual claims should be verified. Also,
GPT-4o is API-based and less customizable but more reliable
for general scientific assistance.
E. Coding and Software Development
Use case: Code generation and review, debugging and
explanation.
Recommendation: DeepSeek is best for logic heavy coding
and on premise use. GPT-4o is preferred for general development support specifically in real-world tools.
Rationale: DeepSeek’s formal reasoning strength translates
into accurate, efficient code especially for complex algorithms.
It performs well on coding benchmarks and can be integrated
into local workflows or fine-tuned on proprietary codebases.
GPT-4o on the other hand powers tools like GitHub Copilot
excelling in practical development with fluent explanations,
meaningful completions and low hallucination rates. It’s great
for code debugging, reviewing and boosting productivity in

[PAGE 16]
IDEs like VS Code. Although GPT-4o lacks customization, it’s
highly effective for everyday development. DeepSeek is well
suited for specialized or offline environments where control
and customization matter the most.
F . High-Stakes Decision Support (Medical, Legal, Financial
advice)
Use case: In such use cases error free suggestions and
precise insights are extremely crucial as there is no room for
the same since they carry serious consequences and domain
knowledge must be spotless and highly accurate.
Recommendation: GPT-4o trumps DeepSeek for highstakes or regulated domains due to its alignment, factual
accuracy, safety filters and refusal to answer when it’s not sure
of the answer. DeepSeek may be used internally but stringent
human oversight is needed.
Rationale: GPT-4o has been evaluated on medical exams
(viz. USMLE) and fine-tuned to smartly handle unsafe advice
and avoid hallucinating the output. It has also been integrated
for medical chatbots with evidence-based responses. In contrast, DeepSeek may hallucinate when unsure which is totally
unacceptable in domains such as medicine, law and finance.
GPT-4o avoids making up legal cases and offers cautious,
well validated answers. DeepSeek may cite plausible but nonexistent laws or provide unqualified financial advice resulting
in lawsuits. For direct user-facing applications GPT-4o is
the go to choice. DeepSeek can support internal workflows
(e.g. doctor notes) but as mentioned in the above notes strict
supervision must be done. In a nutshell, GPT-4o’s safety and
reliability make it the best option for unsupervised use in
domains where the stakes are high and there is no room for
error.
G. Internal Enterprise Applications (Customization and Privacy)
Use case: Deploying LLMs for internal tasks like employee
Q&A, document summarization or knowledge base integration basically scenarios where privacy and customization are
paramount.
Recommendation: Use DeepSeek for on-premise, customizable deployments. Use GPT-4o via Azure OpenAI if
cloud-based privacy and ease of use are enough.
Rationale: Enterprises generally avoid external APIs owing
to confidentiality issues. DeepSeek can run behind a firewall
and fine-tuned on internal data and can be integrated with
company tools. It scales well on enterprise hardware and
offers full control but demands investment in alignment and
monitoring. GPT-4o via Azure keeps data within the tenant’s
cloud and offers some customization but can’t be fine-tuned on
internal documents. For instance, a 1000 pages policy manual
must be reloaded each time. DeepSeek facilitates embedding
that knowledge directly. Enterprises with ML expertise may
choose DeepSeek for control and cost efficiency and others
may opt for GPT-4o for strong support and simplicity.
H. Educational Tools
Use case: AI tutors, language learning apps or similar tools
for students.
Recommendation: GPT-4o is the preferred choice for education due to its safety filters, alignment, consistency and
clarity. DeepSeek is well suitable for offline or cost-sensitive
deployments with proper filtering and time to time supervision
might be necessary.
Rationale: GPT-4o is polite, cautious and avoids inappropriate content best for young learners. It can accurately
identify misconceptions and explain with clarity, clearness
and fluency. DeepSeek has raw knowledge and reasoning
but one needs to heavily supervise the content. Notably,
a recent study [6] compared GPT-4o and DeepSeek-R1 on
the Polish Infectious Diseases Specialty Exam where both
exceeded the passing threshold (60.5%) with DeepSeek-R1
slightly outperforming GPT-4o (73.95% vs 71.43%). This
shows that low-cost open models can match closed models
in healthcare and medical education settings. For language
learning, GPT-4o avoids racial slurs, dangerous or sensitive
topics while DeepSeek may need external moderation and
supervision. Open models like DeepSeek can be fine-tuned to
match academic curriculum and deployed in schools without
internet saving costs but it comes with a cost of additional
complexity. GPT-4o powers trusted and renowned tools like
Duolingo and Khan Academy as it strongly honors reliability,
safety and moderation making it the go to for most educational
apps.
In summary, the choice between GPT-4o and DeepSeek
often boils down to risk vs. control:
• If the priority is safety, reliability and minimal maintenance, GPT-4o is preferred despite its higher cost and
less customizability.
• If the priority is control over data, customization and
cost efficiency then DeepSeek (or similar open models)
is best assuming one can mitigate its weaknesses through
disciplined efforts.
We have seen that each model can potentially excel in
different niches. In fact, some organizations may choose to use
both like for example, use GPT-4o for user-facing functions
and DeepSeek for internal processing or as a fallback option
if API usage is limited. The good news is that the presence
of a strong open model like DeepSeek means users are
not solely dependent on a single company’s model rather
they have multiple options to weigh. This competitive and
complementary dynamic can power further improvements.
V. C ONCLUSION AND FUTURE ROADMAP
A. Summary of Comparison
In this survey, we firstly examined sixteen major challenges
in developing and deploying LLMs and compared how OpenAI’s GPT-4o and DeepSeek tackle each other. Broadly, our
findings highlight a recurring theme:
• GPT-4o represents the culmination of the closed source
approach where investment in huge training costs and

[PAGE 17]
alignment results in a model with breathtaking general
capabilities, refined behavior, robust and sound safety
features. It handles issues such as data quality and model
alignment by applying extensive post training fixes (like
RLHF) and it pushes model performance to new heights
combined with clever optimizations. The result is a stateof-the-art model that is highly reliable in conversation,
factually more accurate than its predecessors and safe
for wide scale implementation due to its built-in content
safeguards. The trade-off is that GPT-4o is a proprietary
system and the AI/ML community does not have deep
insight into its construction which restricts the users from
directly modifying it. We benefit from its strengths but
end of the day we are heavily reliant on OpenAI for any
updates, changes or improvements.
• DeepSeek embodies the open source paradigm where
innovation is shared and efficiency is of foremost priority.
It tackles many challenges not by throwing more compute
indiscriminately but by redesigning the roots of the model
(Mixture-of-Experts for efficiency, new training objectives, etc.) and by leveraging community contributions.
DeepSeek’s major achievement is that they proved that
an open model could manifest cutting edge performance
at a bare minimal cost—an encouraging direction that
LLM development is becoming more accessible and has.
DeepSeek excels in use cases like structured reasoning
and code and offers unmatched transparency i.e. anyone
can check how it works or uses it for their requirements.
However, being open also implies that it lacks the heavy
fine-tuning for safety that GPT-4o provides. Nevertheless,
it is a powerful engine but with fewer guardrails by
default and it leaves more responsibility to the user to
deploy with additional care.
B. Implications for Stakeholders
For AI developers and product owners, these findings mean
that the choice of model should be driven by the specific use
case and context. If you are building a consumer application or
a service where mistakes can cause severe consequences, GPT4o’s extensive safety training provides a level of assurance that
currently justifies its high cost and closed nature.
On the other hand, if you are working on an enterprise tool
where data is confidential or a research project where you
need to fine tune the model, DeepSeek or similar open models
provides you with the freedom but be prepared to implement
additional layers (like safety monitoring and filtering) to use
them responsibly.
For AI researchers, the existence of these two approaches
presents an interesting dual track for progress. Closed models
like GPT-4o push the envelope but often without revealing
how whereas open models like DeepSeek might initially lag
slightly in raw performance but provide the baseline for further
experimentation. One might use DeepSeek to test a hypothesis
about model internals and then assume it likely applies to GPT4. Researchers might also collaborate to fine-tune open models
on alignment and catch up in safe behavior, thus eliminating
some pros of closed models.
For policy makers and ethicists, our comparison demonstrates tangible tradeoffs. Closed models often concentrate
power and capability in the hands of a few firms but those
companies have more control to enforce safety measures—a
centralized approach to mitigating AI risks. Open models
diffuse power, enabling innovation and local control but they
make it harder to enforce consistent safety standards (as
anyone can recklessly deploy the model as they intend to).
C. Future Outlook
This domain is evolving rapidly. Here are some anticipated
future developments and recommendations for the coming
ages:
• Convergence of Techniques: We expect to see techniques pioneered by DeepSeek (e.g. Mixture-of-Experts,
more efficient training algorithms) being adopted in next
gen closed models by organizations (to save high training
costs) and conversely, approaches used by OpenAI for
alignment (RLHF, etc.) being applied more to open
models. This cross pollination could lead to new hybrid
models that are both efficient, safe and well-aligned.
• Specialization vs Generality: One strategy that might
emerge is using a combination of models that a highly
aligned general model (like GPT-4o) as a core and
specialist open models (like one customized especially
for coding) that can be called as needed. There is research
on “mixtures of experts” at the system level (i.e. using
different entire models as experts and a router to pick the
best one for a query). Both OpenAI and open community
may pursue this.
• Evaluation and Trust: The way we evaluate models will
need to become more continuous and dynamic. Instead of
static leaderboards, “evaluation-as-a-service” or “LLM as
a judge” might be the next big thing, where a standardized
set of tests is regularly run on the latest versions of
models. This would help users know how things change
over time.
In conclusion, the landscape of LLMs is characterized by
this interplay between closed source excellence and open
source innovation. Our survey of GPT-4o and DeepSeek
demonstrates that there is no one-works-for-all approach that
single-handedly solves all problems rather each of them provides valuable contributions as a team. Closed models set
benchmarks for capability and safety and open models ensure
that the whole community can learn, contribute and benefit,
preventing AI knowledge from being siloed.
Ideally, the future will bring a synthesis: AI systems that are
not only capable, safe and user friendly as GPT-4o but also
being as transparent, cost efficient, more logical and adaptable
as DeepSeek. Achieving that requires constant collaboration
across industry and open communities, informed by analyses
like this one that compares the strengths and weaknesses of
current models.
