A2FM: An Adaptive Agent Foundation Model for T ool-Aware
Hybrid Reasoning
OPPO AI Agent Team
Abstract
Large language models split into two families: reasoning-centric LLMs, which strengthen internal chainof-thought reasoning but cannot invoke external tools, and agentic LLMs, which learn to interact with
environments and leverage tools but often lag in deep reasoning. This divide arises from fundamentally
different training objectives, leading to mismatched strengths and inefficiency on simple queries, where both
families tend to overthink or over-call tools. In this work, we present Adaptive Agent Foundation Model
(A2FM), a unified framework that follows a route-then-align principle: the model first learns task-aware
routing and then aligns mode-specific trajectories under a shared backbone. To address the inefficiency gap,
we introduce a third mode-instant-that handles simple queries directly, preventing unnecessary reasoning or
tool calls while complementing the agentic and reasoning modes. To jointly enhance accuracy and efficiency,
we propose Adaptive Policy Optimization (APO), which enforces adaptive sampling across modes and
applies a cost-regularized reward. On the 32B scale, A2FM achieves 13.4% on BrowseComp, 70.4% on
AIME25, and 16.7% on HLE, setting new SOTA among comparable models and performing competitively
with frontier LLMs across agentic, reasoning, and general benchmarks. Notably, the adaptive execution
achieves a cost of pass of only $0.00487 per correct answer-cutting cost by 45.2% relative to reasoning and
33.5% relative to agentic, thus delivering substantially higher cost efficiency while maintaining comparable
accuracy.

Their post-training typically optimizes internal deliberation and solution steps. By design, they are trained in closed,
text-only settings and do not learn to invoke external tools. This difference in training objective and environment leaves
a capability gap in tool use.

In parallel, agentic LLMs—e.g., OpenAI GPT-5(OpenAI, 2025a), GLM-4.5 (Zeng et al., 2025), Kimi K2 (Team
et al., 2025a), DeepSeek-V3.1 (DeepSeek-AI, 2025), and agent frameworks such as OAgents (Zhu et al., 2025) and
BrowseMaster (Pang et al., 2025), as well as Agent Foundation Models (Li et al., 2025b)—emphasize environmentdependent interaction. They plan, search, crawl, and execute code. Their post-training focuses on tool correctness, call
patterns, and interaction quality. Yet when deep multi-step reasoning is required (e.g., competitive math), these systems
often trail state-of-the-art reasoning models (Jimenez et al., 2023). Thus, the two families exhibit complementary
strengths: internal CoT versus external tool use.
Recent advances in agent systems and frameworks (Li et al., 2025a; Roucher et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2025; Tao et al.,
2025; Wu et al., 2025b; Zhou et al., 2023b, 2024; Zhu et al., 2025) have greatly expanded the scope of LLM-based
autonomy, enabling complex behaviors such as planning, multi-step tool use, and collaborative decision-making. These
systems often rely on external orchestration—manually designed workflows, multi-agent coordination, or prompt-based
tool pipelines—to decompose tasks and integrate environment feedback. Despite their impressive flexibility, such
designs face several inherent limitations. First, they incur significant computational and latency overhead due to
redundant inter-agent communication and long reasoning traces. Second, their generalization ability is limited: adapting
to new domains typically requires human-engineered prompt flows or redefined agent roles (Zeng et al., 2023; Zhang
et al., 2024). Third, these frameworks depend on general-purpose LLMs that are not trained end-to-end to handle
multi-turn, multi-tool reasoning, preventing them from learning data-driven strategies for mode switching.
Complementary to the above, recent works attempt to blend “thinking” and “non-thinking” behaviors to reduce redundant
computation (Chen et al., 2024b; Fatemi et al., 2025; Kumar et al., 2025; Lou et al., 2025; Sui et al., 2025). However,
these approaches generally operate along a single binary axis—deciding whether to reason or not—without the capacity
to adaptively invokeagenticbehaviors such as tool use and task planning. As a result, while they can shorten overextended reasoning traces, they remain unable to manage the broader spectrum of reasoning–acting trade-offs inherent
to real-world tasks. Moreover, introducing agentic behaviors further compounds this limitation by increasing runtime
and inflating the overallcost of pass(Erol et al., 2025).
To address the challenges mentioned above, we proposeA 2FM (Adaptive Agent Foundation Model), which unifies three
complementary modes within a single backbone -agentic(tool-aware action),reasoning(explicit CoT), andinstant
(direct answer) - thereby bridging the gap between reasoning-centric LLMs and orchestrated agent systems. To bridge
the capability gap, A2FM internalizes a self-adaptive router that learnswhat to doper query, naturally combining the
strengths of reasoning and agentic paradigms in one model. To address efficiency, we add theinstantmode so that
simple queries are answered directly, avoiding unnecessary reasoning or tool calls. Supervised fine-tuning follows a

[PAGE 4]
route-then-alignprinciple: the model first performs task-aware routing, then aligns mode-conditioned trajectories under
a shared backbone using diverse datasets spanning tool use, explicit reasoning, and direct answering.
Nevertheless, simply mixing modes falls short: the model must not only maintain high accuracy but also minimize
computational cost, yet boundary queries remain difficult to route and data often underutilized. Prior work either omits
explicit modeling of mode mixing (Jiang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025b), or focuses solely on efficiency by shortening
reasoning traces in post-training (Arora and Zanette, 2025; Team et al., 2025b), or fails to jointly optimize routing
and trajectory generation in an end-to-end framework (Wu et al., 2025a). We therefore introduceAdaptive Policy
Optimization (APO), a reinforcement learning procedure tailored for mode selection. APO builds on group-relative
policy optimization (Shao et al., 2024), enforces adaptive sampling across modes to prevent collapse, and applies
a dynamic, cost-regularized reward (with LLM-as-Judge signals (Zheng et al., 2023)) that balances accuracy with
efficiency. It explicitly favors minimal-effortinstantsolutions on easy queries while escalating toreasoningoragentic
modes when external evidence or extended deliberation is needed.
Empirically, A2FM achieves state-of-the-art results across benchmarks: on agentic tasks it obtains 13.4% on BrowseComp, on reasoning it reaches 70.4% on AIME25, and on general tasks it delivers 16.7% on HLE. Beyond raw accuracy,
APO substantially improves efficiency: on SuperGPQA, the adaptive execution yields a cost of pass of only$0.00487
per correct answer—cutting cost by45.2%relative to reasoning and33.5%relative to agentic—while maintaining
comparable accuracy.
In summary, our key contributions include:
• We present A2FM, the first adaptive agent foundation model that integratesagentic,reasoning, andinstantmodes
under a single backbone via a self-adaptive router.
• We propose A two-stage process: (i) supervised route-then-align fine-tuning for mode-conditioned trajectories; (ii)
APO for mode selection with adaptive sampling and cost-regularized rewards.
• We empirically demonstrate that A2FM achieves state-of-the-art results at the 32B scale across agentic, reasoning, and
general benchmarks, with substantial reductions in token usage and computation versus mode-forcing baselines.
2 Related Work
2.1 Agent Systems and Frameworks
Recent agent systems extend large language model (LLM) autonomy beyond text-only reasoning by introducing
planning, multi-step tool use, and collaborative decision-making pipelines (Hu et al., 2025; Jin et al., 2025b; Mai
et al., 2025; Roucher et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2023b, 2024). A common design pattern involves
external orchestration: the system decomposes tasks into subgoals, assigns them to role-specialized agents such as
planners, researchers, coders, and verifiers, and coordinates calls to search, browsing, and code-execution tools. These
frameworks demonstrate strong performance on complex, open-ended problems through explicit workflow design and
tool integration (Chen et al., 2023; Fourney et al., 2024; Hong et al., 2024; Li et al., 2023; Qian et al., 2023).
Within this paradigm, multi-agent frameworks leverage division of labor and tool diversity to improve coordination and
task efficiency (Roucher et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2025; Xie et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2023b, 2024).
However, they often exhibit high computational and latency overhead due to inter-agent communication and extended
reasoning traces, and generalization to new domains typically requires additional prompt or workflow engineering (Hu
et al., 2025; Zeng et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024). The backbone LLMs in these systems are not trained end-to-end
for multi-turn, multi-tool protocols, and therefore rely on predefined orchestration rules rather than learned decision
policies (Li et al., 2025b; Liu et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2023a).
Single- and few-agent frameworks focus on tool-centric and deep-search tasks with tighter reasoning–action loops.
Representative examples include WebDancer (Wu et al., 2025b), WebShaper (Tao et al., 2025), WebSailor (Li et al.,
2025a), Asearcher(Gao et al., 2025), and AFM (Li et al., 2025b), which emphasize structured browsing, information
extraction, and verification, as well as OAgents (Zhu et al., 2025) and DeepResearcher (Zheng et al., 2025), which
integrate planning and tool APIs based on strong proprietary backbones. These systems enhance reliability in search
and code-execution tasks and introduce structured planning procedures such as step-wise verification or retry mechanisms (Gao et al., 2025; Pang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025a). Nevertheless, they are largely governed by controller

[PAGE 5]
heuristics and fixed procedural flows, where routing among direct answering, internal reasoning, or external tool use is
manually designed rather than adaptively learned from data (Li et al., 2025d; Qian et al., 2025; Xue et al., 2025).
2.2 Adaptive Reasoning Approaches
A growing line of work improves reasoning efficiency by letting models adaptively regulate how long and when to
think, rather than relying on fixed, full-length chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning. These methods broadly fall into two
complementary categories: length-aware control, which optimizes the depth of reasoning, and capability-aware routing,
which decides when extended reasoning should be invoked.
2.2.1 Length-Aware Control
Length-aware methods reduce redundant token consumption by training models to produce concise CoT traces when fullstep reasoning is unnecessary. Two main paradigms are common: (1) reinforcement learning with length regularization,
which introduces token-length penalties or efficiency terms to balance accuracy and brevity (Aggarwal and Welleck,
2025; Arora and Zanette, 2025; Yeo et al., 2025); and (2) supervised CoT compression, which yields variable-length CoT
via post-hoc summarization or by learning compressed reasoning patterns from data (Kang et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024;
Munkhbat et al., 2025; Xia et al., 2025). These approaches demonstrate consistent token savings while maintaining task
performance.
2.2.2 Capability-Aware Routing
Capability-aware routing focuses on when to reason by using internal signals (e.g., uncertainty, logit margins, hidden
representations) to estimate difficulty and trigger appropriate reasoning depth (Chen et al., 2024a; He et al., 2025).
Several works further adopt reinforcement learning to learn when-to-think policies (Lou et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025);
Bimodal Policy Optimization contrasts utilities of reasoning vs. non-reasoning trajectories for the same input (Yang
et al., 2025b); and Large Hybrid Reasoning Models route by query semantics to invoke extended reasoning when
beneficial (Jiang et al., 2025). Overall, this literature primarily studies binary switching between short and long CoT and
generally does not incorporate end-to-end training for adaptive tool use.

3.2 Stage 1: Route-then-Align Fine-tuning
Training Data Generation.The first stage centers on supervised route-then-align training, where the model learns
to classify a query into one of three modes— instant, reasoning, or agentic—and then generate mode-consistent
trajectories. Mode routing is triggered by paired <classification> tags, after which the model proceeds with different
behaviors:
- In theinstantmode, the model directly outputs the final prediction within<answer>tags, minimizing deliberation.
- In the reasoning mode, the model provides a chain-of-thought wrapped in <reasoning> tags, followed by the final
result in<answer>tags.
- In the agentic mode, the model interleaves high-level reasoning with external tool usage. While inspired by Agent
Foundation Models (Li et al., 2025b), our design departs significantly in howplanandsummaryare used.
•plan : appears only once at the beginning, decomposing the query into multiple sub-goals that can be executed in
parallel.
•summary : operates dynamically across the process, allowing the system to concurrently aggregate solved
sub-tasks, terminate completed threads, and open new ones when needed.
This explicitly parallel architecture enables simultaneous multi-tool execution, substantially improving both efficiency
and effectiveness of tool usage.
Environment Interaction (Agentic Mode).To support agentic execution, the policy interacts with external resources
through three MCP-style tool interfaces that return structured observations:
•web_search : We interface with the Google Search engine for information retrieval, conducting web searches via
the SerpAPI1 tool. The core SerpAPI configuration parameters are the search query string and the number of
results to return. In practice, the tool issues model-generated queries and, by default, retrieves the top 5 results for
each query. Each result includes a title, a snippet, and the corresponding Uniform Resource Locator (URL). This
setup provides essential support for subsequent analysis and decision-making processes.
•crawl_page : We employed a web-crawling tool authenticated via Jina API2 and summarized the retrieved content
using gpt-5-mini. The tool’s core configuration parameters comprise the Uniform Resource Locator (URL), the
summary query. Candidate URLs are generated by the model, and the tool uses the Jina API to crawl each URL.
Subsequently, we invoke the gpt-5-mini model to produce a summary for each crawled page; the summary prompt
is provided in Appendix A.2.
•code_execute : To ensure usability and security, we realize the code sandbox with nsjail3, a lightweight utility
that provisions isolated execution environments for Python code. By leveraging Linux namespace isolation, nsjail
hardens file-system boundaries and prevents unauthorized access to host resources. A notable strength of this
approach is its compatibility with containerized ecosystems (e.g., Docker), which facilitates seamless migration
across diverse training and testing settings. The tool also offers fine-grained resource controls. during the training
process, we cap CPU time at 5 seconds and memory at 5 GB to keep code execution strictly bounded.
Unless otherwise noted, tokens originating from <tool_response> are treated as observations and excluded from the
optimization loss; the model is trained only on its own generated tokens, so learning focuses on routing, reasoning, and
action selection rather than memorizing tool outputs.
The agentic trajectory begins with a <plan> section, executes N tools in parallel (each wrapped in <tool_call> tags),
and collects their results within <tool_response> tags. These tool results are masked during training—following the

Data Curation.To construct diverse and challenging training data, we employ two complementary heuristics. First, a
difficulty-based sampling strategy adjusts the natural distribution of task success. In the raw dataset, sampling typically
yields a U-shaped distribution—tasks are either consistently solved or consistently unsolved, with few of intermediate
difficulty. To enhance the representation of moderately challenging cases, we deliberately downsample “always-solved”
instances, resulting in a J-shaped distribution that better balances task difficulty (see Fig. 3). This modification ensures
broader coverage across the difficulty spectrum and exposes the model to queries near its decision boundaries. Second,
we explicitly address classification-ambiguous queries where routing decisions are uncertain. Instead of relying on
majority votes, we assign the mode label corresponding to the trajectory that achieves the highest accuracy on that query.
This encourages stronger supervision near ambiguous boundaries, improving the model’s ability to discriminate between
reasoning, agentic, and instant behaviors when multiple modes appear plausible.
For distillation, we adopt mode-specialized teachers: DeepSeek R1 (Guo et al., 2025) (strong reasoning capability) for
the reasoning mode, and DeepSeek V3.1 (DeepSeek-AI, 2025) (broad general competence) for the agentic and instant
modes. This complementary setup ensures that each mode benefits from the most suitable teacher, leading to more
effective supervision and consistent alignment under a unified backbone.

3.3 Stage2: Adaptive Policy Optimization
Aiming to tackle the challenges in balancing accuracy and efficiency in query processing, we propose Adaptive Policy
Optimization (APO), which equips the model with dynamic mode-selection—choosing among agentic, reasoning, or
instant—based on task demands.
APO builds on GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) but extends it in two key ways: (i) a tailored rollout strategy that ensures
sufficient exploration of all modes, avoiding under-sampling; and (ii) an adaptive reward that explicitly encodes the
accuracy–efficiency trade-off, rather than relying on coarse binary rewards. This design enables the model to not only
maintain correctness but also minimize unnecessary computation, achieving accurate yet cost-effective routing.

classification tag is inserted at the beginning of the model’s response. This guarantees that every query is explored

4 Experiment
4.1 Dataset Composition
Our dataset sources encompass a variety of task types and domains. We specifically chose not to focus on easy datasets but
rather to select data from more challenging and complex sources. These include datasets such as Webdancer(Wu et al., 2025b),
Webshaper(Tao et al., 2025), Taskcraft(Shi et al., 2025), 2Wiki(Ho et al., 2020), MusiqueHo et al. (2020), and HotpotQA(Yang et al.,
2018) for agentic-related tasks, and mathematic reasoning datasets like Skywork-Math(Zeng et al., 2024), DAPO-Math-17K(Yu
et al., 2025), and GSM8K(Cobbe et al., 2021). Additionally, code-related data sources like Skywork-Code(Zeng et al., 2024) and
Codeforces(Penedo et al., 2025) were incorporated for programming tasks. Importantly, while we did not curate separate datasets
solely for the instant mode, we deliberately retained relatively simple examples within the search and reasoning datasets. This ensures
balance across the three modes and allows the model to naturally learn to apply the instant mode on straightforward tasks. Finally, we
created 5289, 2829, and 2890 trajectories for agentic, reasoning and instant modes respectively during the route-then-align SFT phase
and 6000 question-answer pairs during the APO phase.
4.2 Experimental Setup
We train A2FM on a mixture of agentic, reasoning, and code-related datasets, while also retaining relatively simple cases to enable
learning of the instant mode. Full dataset composition and sampling strategy are provided in Appendix 4.1.
Baselines.For comparison, we conducted a comprehensive evaluation of our trained model against three categories of systems: (1)
general-purpose large language models (LLMs), including closed-source models such as GPT-4.1(OpenAI, 2025b), o1 (Jaech et al.,

2024), Claude-4-Sonnet(Anthropic, 2025), and open-source models DeepSeek-R1(Guo et al., 2025), Qwen2.5-Instruct-32B (Yang

et al., 2025); and (3) Agent Foundation Models, including DeepDive(Lu et al., 2025), WebDancer(Wu et al., 2025b), WebSailor(Li

Implementation Details.We conduct all experiments on the Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct backbone. In the route-then-align SFT stage,
we train for 3 epochs with a batch size of 256, AdamW optimizer, cosine decay learning rate schedule, and a max sequence length
of 32,768. In the APO stage, we train for 2 epochs with a learning rate of 1e-6, 5 warmup steps, batch size 128, and 12 rollouts
per prompt (with ρ=3 and γ=3 ) capped at 65,536 tokens. We set α=2 for adaptive reward calculation. For inference, we set
temperature 1.0, top-p 0.9, top-k 20, and a max output length of 131,072.

Approaching the Pareto Frontier under APO.Figure 4 illustrates how A 2FM evolves under APO training, evaluated across
all benchmarks except AIME24/25, since these tasks are routed entirely into the reasoning mode and offer no meaningful trade-off
with instant responses. The three yellow stars mark checkpoints at step 10, 30, and 50, showing a clear trajectory where the adaptive
model steadily reduces non-instant usage while improving accuracy. As training progresses, A2FM moves closer to the Pareto frontier
of accuracy–efficiency trade-offs, indicating that APO effectively teaches the router to allocate more queries to the instant mode
without sacrificing correctness.
At convergence, A2FM (RL) achieves an average accuracy of53.8%with a non-instant ratio of77.1%, compared to the “Best Mode”
oracle (accuracy 55.4%, non-instant ratio 100%). Although the accuracy gap is marginal (–1.6 points), our adaptive model lowers
non-instant triggering by22.9points, demonstrating that APO attains near-oracle accuracy while substantially improving efficiency.

Robustness of Adaptive Routing on Complex Tasks.We compare adaptive routing with forced single-mode execution
(agentic, reasoning, and near-instant) across four benchmarks: GAIA, XBench-DeepSearch, AIME24, and SuperGPQA. GAIA

Overall, adaptive mode provides robust performance while retaining flexibility across diverse task types.
6 Conclusion
We present A2FM, an Adaptive Agent Foundation Model that unifies agentic, reasoning, and instant modes under a single backbone
with a self-adaptive router. By combining route-then-align training with Adaptive Policy Optimization, A2FM achieves both high
accuracy and efficiency, matching or surpassing strong baselines across agentic, reasoning, and general benchmarks. Our results
highlight the promise of adaptive multi-mode modeling as a scalable path toward efficient and versatile LLM agents.

SYSTEM PROMPT OFA 2FM
You are required to solve the task by using one of the three agent options: agentic_agent, reasoning_agent, and instant_agent.
1.Agent Options:
(a) agentic_agent: choose this agent if the task needs tosearchandcrawlreal-world / factual information (such as news
and data) orexecuting code(such as programming tasks, data processing or statistics).
